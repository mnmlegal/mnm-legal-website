Regular Bail in India: Legal Framework Under the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023

Understanding release from custody, statutory bail rights, judicial discretion, default bail, undertrial detention, bail bonds and criminal court procedure.

MNM Legal Associates Criminal Law Legal Guide

Personal liberty is a fundamental constitutional protection guaranteed under Article 21 of the Constitution of India.

Arrest and detention during criminal proceedings do not amount to a determination of guilt. Subject to applicable statutory provisions, a person in custody may seek release on bail.

The law governing bail seeks to balance individual liberty, the presumption of innocence, the requirements of criminal investigation and the proper administration of justice.

1. What Is Regular Bail?

Regular bail refers to the release of a person from custody in connection with a criminal case, subject to applicable statutory provisions and conditions imposed by the competent authority or court.

Unlike anticipatory bail, which provides protection in the event of arrest, regular bail ordinarily concerns a person who has already been arrested or has submitted to the custody of the court.

The grant of bail permits the accused to remain outside custody while complying with the requirements of investigation and criminal proceedings.

Bail Is Not Acquittal

Grant of bail does not establish innocence, terminate criminal proceedings or result in automatic discharge or acquittal. The criminal case continues in accordance with law.

2. Statutory Framework Under BNSS

The Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 came into force on 1 July 2024, replacing the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, subject to applicable transitional and savings provisions.

Subject BNSS, 2023 CrPC, 1973
Bail in bailable offences Section 478 Section 436
Maximum undertrial detention Section 479 Section 436A
Bail in non-bailable offences Section 480 Section 437
Anticipatory bail Section 482 Section 438
Special powers of superior courts Section 483 Section 439
Default bail Section 187(3) Section 167(2)
Amount and reduction of bond Section 484 Section 440

The corresponding provisions under the former CrPC remain relevant where applicable under the savings provisions and for understanding established judicial precedents.

Section 531 BNSS governs repeal and savings. The applicable procedural framework must be determined with reference to the relevant proceedings and transitional requirements.

3. Bail in Bailable Offences — Section 478 BNSS

In a bailable offence, release on bail is ordinarily a statutory right rather than a matter of unrestricted judicial discretion.

Section 478 BNSS provides for release on bail where a person accused of a bailable offence is arrested or detained without warrant by an officer in charge of a police station, or appears or is brought before a court, and is prepared to furnish bail.

Release of an Indigent Accused

Where an accused person is indigent and unable to furnish surety, Section 478 provides for discharge upon execution of a bond for appearance, subject to the statutory requirements.

Inability to furnish bail within one week of arrest is sufficient ground for the officer or court to presume that the person is indigent for the purpose of the provision.

Bailable Offence Does Not Mean No Arrest

Classification of an offence as bailable concerns the entitlement to bail. It does not, by itself, prohibit a lawful arrest. Arrest and bail are distinct legal questions.

The entitlement remains subject to applicable statutory exceptions, including circumstances contemplated under Section 478(2).

4. Bail in Non-Bailable Offences — Section 480 BNSS

A non-bailable offence is not an offence for which bail is necessarily prohibited. Rather, grant of ordinary bail is generally governed by judicial discretion and applicable statutory restrictions.

Section 480 BNSS regulates the grant of bail in non-bailable cases by the authorities and courts specified in that provision, other than the High Court and Court of Session.

Statutory Restrictions

Section 480 imposes restrictions in specified circumstances, including cases where reasonable grounds exist for believing that the accused has committed an offence punishable with death or imprisonment for life.

Additional restrictions may apply in cases involving specified previous convictions and criminal antecedents.

Special Considerations

The provision recognises specified exceptions and considerations concerning children, women, sick or infirm persons, subject to the applicable statutory requirements.

These considerations do not create an unconditional entitlement to bail in every case.

Delay in Trial Before a Magistrate

Section 480(6) addresses cases involving non-bailable offences triable by a Magistrate where the trial is not concluded within sixty days from the first date fixed for taking evidence and the accused has remained in custody throughout that period.

The provision contemplates release on bail unless the Magistrate directs otherwise for reasons recorded in writing.

5. Which Court Can Grant Regular Bail?

The appropriate authority or court depends upon the nature of the offence, the applicable statute, the stage of proceedings and the jurisdiction governing the criminal case.

  • Police authorities in circumstances permitted by law.
  • Jurisdictional Magistrate.
  • Court of Session.
  • High Court.
  • Designated Special Court where required under a special enactment.

Section 483 BNSS

Section 483 confers special powers upon the High Court and Court of Session to grant bail to an accused person in custody, impose appropriate conditions and exercise other powers specified in the provision.

These powers remain subject to applicable statutory requirements, including notice and participation requirements in specified cases.

Regular Bail Proceedings in Ahmedabad

For criminal cases arising in Ahmedabad, the appropriate Magistrate, Sessions Court or Special Court must be identified with reference to the concerned police station, alleged offences and territorial jurisdiction.

The High Court of Gujarat may exercise its bail jurisdiction in accordance with law.

6. Grounds Considered While Granting Bail

In cases involving discretionary bail, courts examine the relevant facts and circumstances without ordinarily conducting a detailed trial of the allegations at the bail stage.

Relevant considerations may include:

  • Nature and gravity of the alleged offence.
  • Specific role attributed to the accused.
  • Prima facie material supporting the allegations.
  • Severity of the prescribed punishment.
  • Length of detention already undergone.
  • Stage and progress of investigation.
  • Possibility of absconding.
  • Risk of tampering with evidence.
  • Possibility of influencing or intimidating witnesses.
  • Previous criminal antecedents.
  • Likelihood of repetition of the alleged offence.
  • Medical condition and other relevant personal circumstances.
  • Parity with similarly situated co-accused persons.
  • Likelihood of substantial delay in completion of trial.

Completion of Investigation

Completion of investigation and filing of the police report may constitute relevant considerations in assessing the necessity of continued detention.

However, filing of a charge sheet does not automatically create an entitlement to ordinary discretionary bail.

Parity With Co-Accused

Where a similarly situated co-accused has been granted bail, the principle of parity may be relevant.

The court must nevertheless examine the respective roles, allegations, antecedents and individual circumstances before applying the principle.

7. Regular Bail Application Procedure

  1. Examination of Criminal Case The FIR, allegations, arrest details, remand orders and applicable statutory provisions are examined.
  2. Identification of Appropriate Court The competent court is identified with reference to the offence, territorial jurisdiction and applicable special laws.
  3. Preparation of Bail Application Relevant facts, legal grounds, custody details, previous proceedings and supporting documents are incorporated in the application.
  4. Filing Before Competent Court The application is filed in accordance with applicable court rules and procedural requirements.
  5. Response of Prosecution The prosecution and other persons entitled to participate under applicable law are afforded the required opportunity to respond.
  6. Hearing and Judicial Determination The court considers the submissions, relevant material and applicable statutory requirements.
  7. Execution of Bail Bond Where bail is granted, the prescribed bond, surety or other lawful release requirements must be satisfied.
  8. Release From Custody Release is effected in accordance with the judicial order and applicable procedure, provided detention is not required in another matter.

8. Default Bail Under Section 187(3) BNSS

Default bail, also known as statutory bail, arises from the failure to complete the investigation and file the requisite police report within the applicable statutory period.

Section 187(3) BNSS prescribes different maximum periods of detention during investigation, depending upon the nature of the offence.

Category of Offence Statutory Period
Offences punishable with death, imprisonment for life, or imprisonment for a term of ten years or more. 90 days
Other offences. 60 days

Upon expiry of the applicable period, an accused person may become entitled to default bail if the statutory requirements are satisfied and the right is duly exercised.

The accused must be prepared to furnish bail in accordance with law.

Special Statutes and Extended Investigation Periods

Certain special enactments provide different investigation periods or permit extensions subject to prescribed judicial safeguards. The general sixty-day and ninety-day periods must not be applied mechanically to every criminal case.

When Should the Right Be Exercised?

Where the right to default bail has accrued, it must ordinarily be exercised before filing of the requisite police report.

A subsequent filing of the police report does not ordinarily defeat a right that has already been validly exercised in accordance with law.

Calculation of the statutory period and determination of entitlement require examination of the relevant remand orders, applicable enactment and procedural history.

9. Maximum Period of Undertrial Detention — Section 479 BNSS

Section 479 BNSS addresses prolonged detention of undertrial prisoners during investigation, inquiry or trial.

Subject to statutory exclusions and conditions, an undertrial prisoner who has undergone detention extending to one-half of the maximum period of imprisonment prescribed for the offence shall be released on bail by the court.

First-Time Offenders

The provision further recognises that a first-time offender, meaning a person who has never been convicted of any offence in the past, shall be released on bond after undergoing detention extending to one-third of the maximum prescribed imprisonment, subject to the statutory framework.

Category General Threshold
Eligible undertrial prisoner One-half of maximum prescribed imprisonment
Eligible first-time offender One-third of maximum prescribed imprisonment

Restrictions and Exceptions

Section 479 contains important exclusions and qualifications, including those concerning offences for which death or imprisonment for life is specified as one of the punishments.

The provision also addresses cases involving multiple offences or multiple pending cases, delay attributable to the accused and circumstances in which continued detention may be directed in accordance with law.

The Superintendent of the jail is required to make an application to the court upon completion of the relevant detention period as contemplated under Section 479(3).

Default Bail and Undertrial Release Are Distinct

Default bail under Section 187(3) concerns expiry of the applicable investigation period. Section 479 concerns the duration of undertrial detention relative to the prescribed punishment and applicable statutory conditions.

10. Conditions That May Be Imposed

A competent court may impose lawful conditions while granting bail in accordance with the applicable statutory provisions and circumstances of the case.

Such conditions may include:

  • Attending court proceedings as required.
  • Cooperating with lawful investigation.
  • Not committing a similar offence.
  • Not tampering with evidence.
  • Not threatening or influencing witnesses.
  • Complying with lawful travel restrictions, where imposed.
  • Furnishing the prescribed bond or surety.
  • Informing the court of relevant changes in address, where directed.

Bail conditions should be proportionate, legally sustainable and connected with securing the administration of justice.

Conditions should not be so excessive or onerous that they effectively defeat the grant of bail.

11. Bail Bonds, Personal Bonds and Sureties

A bail bond is a legal undertaking associated with release on bail and compliance with the requirements of appearance before the competent court.

Personal Bond

A personal bond is an undertaking executed by the accused without a surety, where release on such a bond is permitted or directed under the applicable law.

Surety

A surety undertakes responsibility in accordance with the terms of the bail bond and may incur liability upon forfeiture in accordance with law.

Amount of Bail Bond

Section 484 BNSS provides that the amount of every bond shall be fixed with due regard to the circumstances of the case and shall not be excessive.

The High Court or Court of Session may exercise the power to reduce bail required by a police officer or Magistrate in accordance with the statutory provision.

Grant of Bail and Actual Release

A judicial order granting bail may require completion of bond and surety formalities before actual release from custody. Detention in another criminal matter may independently prevent release.

12. Bail Under Special Criminal Statutes

Certain special enactments prescribe additional restrictions, special procedures or stringent requirements governing the grant of bail.

Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances Act, 1985

Section 37 NDPS Act imposes additional bail restrictions in cases involving specified offences and offences involving commercial quantity.

Where applicable, the statutory requirements include an opportunity for the Public Prosecutor to oppose the application and prescribed findings by the court where the application is opposed.

Prevention of Money Laundering Act, 2002

Section 45 PMLA prescribes additional conditions governing bail in cases falling within the provision, subject to applicable exceptions and judicial interpretation.

Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act, 1967

Section 43D(5) UAPA imposes special bail restrictions concerning specified offences under Chapters IV and VI of the enactment.

Protection of Children From Sexual Offences Act, 2012

Bail proceedings involving offences under the POCSO Act require consideration of the applicable statutory framework, designated court jurisdiction and safeguards concerning the child.

Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989

Regular bail proceedings under the SC/ST Act may involve special court jurisdiction, victim participation rights and the appellate framework under Section 14A.

The precise statutory provisions and applicable judicial precedents must be examined separately in every such case.

13. What Happens if Regular Bail Is Rejected?

Rejection of a bail application does not amount to a finding that the accused is guilty.

Depending upon the applicable statutory framework and the court that passed the order, the accused may approach the competent superior court or pursue another remedy available under law.

Successive Bail Applications

A subsequent bail application may be maintainable where a genuine change in circumstances has occurred.

Relevant developments may include completion of investigation, examination of material witnesses, substantial delay in trial, deterioration of health or other legally relevant circumstances.

Previous bail applications and judicial orders must be accurately disclosed before the court.

14. Cancellation of Regular Bail

Bail may be cancelled by a competent court in accordance with the applicable statutory provisions and judicial principles.

Relevant circumstances may include:

  • Violation of material bail conditions.
  • Absconding or evasion of judicial proceedings.
  • Interference with investigation.
  • Intimidation or unlawful influence upon witnesses.
  • Tampering with evidence.
  • Misuse of liberty granted by the court.
  • Other legally recognised grounds.

Cancellation of bail due to subsequent misconduct must be distinguished from setting aside a bail order that was legally unsustainable when originally granted.

Sections 480(5) and 483(3) BNSS contain relevant powers concerning arrest and commitment to custody of persons released on bail.

16. Documents Commonly Required

Documentary requirements depend upon the circumstances of the criminal case and applicable court rules.

  • Copy of the FIR.
  • Arrest memo or available arrest particulars.
  • Remand applications and remand orders.
  • Relevant police report or charge sheet, where available.
  • Previous bail applications and judicial orders.
  • Identity and address documents, where required.
  • Relevant supporting documentary material.
  • Medical records, where applicable.
  • Orders granting bail to similarly situated co-accused, where relevant.
  • Vakalatnama, affidavit and other documents required under court rules.
  • Surety documents required for compliance with the bail order.

All relevant facts, previous applications and judicial proceedings should be accurately disclosed.

17. Landmark Supreme Court Judgments

The following decisions explain important principles governing bail, personal liberty, statutory entitlement and judicial discretion.

State of Rajasthan v. Balchand

(1977) 4 SCC 308

The Supreme Court articulated the principle that bail is ordinarily the rule and imprisonment the exception, subject to legally relevant circumstances.

Gudikanti Narasimhulu v. Public Prosecutor, High Court of Andhra Pradesh

(1978) 1 SCC 240

The Court examined the relationship between personal liberty, judicial discretion and the administration of criminal justice in bail proceedings.

Moti Ram v. State of Madhya Pradesh

(1978) 4 SCC 47

The Court considered excessive bail requirements, financial circumstances of accused persons and the importance of meaningful access to release on bail.

Hussainara Khatoon (I) v. Home Secretary, State of Bihar

(1980) 1 SCC 81

The Court recognised speedy trial as an essential component of constitutional protection under Article 21 and addressed prolonged undertrial detention.

Sanjay Chandra v. Central Bureau of Investigation

(2012) 1 SCC 40

The Supreme Court emphasised that the object of bail is to secure the appearance of the accused at trial and that pre-trial detention should not ordinarily operate as punishment.

Dataram Singh v. State of Uttar Pradesh

(2018) 3 SCC 22

The Court reiterated the importance of the presumption of innocence and a judicious approach to the grant or refusal of bail.

Prasanta Kumar Sarkar v. Ashis Chatterjee

(2010) 14 SCC 496

The Supreme Court identified relevant considerations governing bail, including the nature of accusations, gravity of the offence, risk of absconding and possibility of interference with witnesses.

Satender Kumar Antil v. Central Bureau of Investigation

(2022) 10 SCC 51

The Supreme Court examined unnecessary arrests, procedural safeguards, bail administration and the need to protect personal liberty within the criminal justice system.

M. Ravindran v. Intelligence Officer, Directorate of Revenue Intelligence

(2021) 2 SCC 485

The Supreme Court explained the accrual and exercise of the statutory right to default bail and the effect of subsequent filing of the complaint or prosecution material.

Union of India v. K.A. Najeeb

(2021) 3 SCC 713

The Court recognised the importance of constitutional protections in cases involving prolonged incarceration and substantial delay in trial, notwithstanding statutory restrictions under special laws.

Dolat Ram v. State of Haryana

(1995) 1 SCC 349

The Court distinguished rejection of bail at the initial stage from cancellation of bail already granted and examined the circumstances justifying cancellation.

Application of Judicial Precedents

Judicial decisions must be interpreted in their factual and statutory context. Earlier decisions under the CrPC remain relevant insofar as their principles are consistent with the applicable BNSS provisions and subsequent judicial developments.

18. Frequently Asked Questions

Can regular bail be sought immediately after arrest?

Yes. An arrested person may seek bail before the appropriate authority or court in accordance with the applicable statutory provisions.

Is bail a matter of right in a bailable offence?

Ordinarily, yes. Section 478 BNSS provides a statutory entitlement to bail in bailable offences, subject to applicable requirements and exceptions.

Can bail be granted in a non-bailable offence?

Yes. Non-bailable classification does not necessarily prohibit bail. The court considers the applicable law, allegations, circumstances and statutory restrictions.

Does filing a charge sheet guarantee regular bail?

No. Completion of investigation may be relevant, but filing a charge sheet does not automatically create an entitlement to ordinary discretionary bail.

What is default bail?

Default bail is a statutory entitlement arising upon expiry of the applicable investigation period where the prescribed legal requirements are satisfied.

What is the difference between sixty-day and ninety-day default bail?

Section 187(3) BNSS prescribes different investigation periods according to the nature and punishment of the alleged offence. Special statutes may provide different periods or extensions.

Can an accused be released without a surety?

Release on a personal bond may be permissible in appropriate circumstances, subject to the applicable statutory provisions and judicial order.

Can bail be granted on medical grounds?

Medical circumstances may be relevant to the determination of bail. The court considers the medical material, nature of the offence and applicable law.

Can regular bail be granted in an NDPS case?

Yes, where the applicable legal requirements are satisfied. Section 37 NDPS Act imposes stringent conditions in specified categories of offences.

Can another bail application be filed after rejection?

A subsequent application may be maintainable in appropriate circumstances, particularly where a genuine change in circumstances has occurred. Previous orders must be disclosed.

Can bail be cancelled after release?

Yes. A competent court may cancel bail or set aside an improper bail order on legally recognised grounds.

Does regular bail end the criminal case?

No. Bail concerns release from custody. Investigation, inquiry and trial may continue in accordance with law.

How long does a regular bail application take?

There is no uniform disposal period applicable to every bail application. The applicable statutory requirements, court procedure, urgency and circumstances of the case must be considered.

Does a bail order automatically result in immediate release?

Actual release may require execution of the prescribed bond, verification of sureties and completion of release formalities. Detention in another criminal matter may independently prevent release.
