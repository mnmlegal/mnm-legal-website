Cheque Bounce Law in India: Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act

Understanding cheque dishonour, statutory demand notices, limitation periods, territorial jurisdiction, compensation and legal proceedings under Indian law.

MNM Legal Associates Negotiable Instruments Law Legal Guide

Cheques continue to be used in India for commercial transactions, repayment of loans, settlement of financial obligations and other lawful payments.

Where a cheque issued towards a legally enforceable debt or liability is dishonoured, the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881 may provide a statutory criminal remedy, subject to compliance with the prescribed legal requirements.

Cheque dishonour proceedings involve strict statutory timelines, documentary evidence, presumptions of law and procedural requirements. Both the complainant and the accused have rights and obligations under the Act.

1. What Is Cheque Dishonour?

A cheque is dishonoured when the bank refuses to make payment upon presentation of the cheque in accordance with applicable banking requirements.

Dishonour is commonly referred to as a cheque bounce.

Common Reasons for Cheque Dishonour

  • Insufficient funds.
  • Amount exceeding the agreed arrangement.
  • Closure of the bank account.
  • Stop-payment instructions.
  • Signature mismatch.
  • Material alteration of the cheque.
  • Presentation after expiry of validity.
  • Other banking or technical reasons.
Every Dishonoured Cheque Is Not Automatically an Offence

Criminal liability under Section 138 arises only when the statutory ingredients are satisfied. The reason for dishonour, existence of a legally enforceable liability and compliance with the statutory procedure must be examined.

2. Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act

Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881 creates a statutory offence in specified circumstances involving dishonour of a cheque issued towards discharge, wholly or partly, of a legally enforceable debt or other liability.

The provision is intended to promote reliability in cheque-based transactions and provide a legal mechanism for addressing qualifying instances of cheque dishonour.

Judicial interpretation has recognised that certain reasons for dishonour, including account closure and stop-payment instructions, may also attract Section 138 where the other statutory requirements are established.

Relevant judicial authorities include Modi Cements Ltd. v. Kuchil Kumar Nandi, (1998) 3 SCC 249 and Laxmi Dyechem v. State of Gujarat, (2012) 13 SCC 375.

3. Essential Ingredients of a Section 138 Offence

The following statutory requirements must ordinarily be established:

  1. Issuance of Cheque The cheque must have been drawn by a person on an account maintained by that person with a banker.
  2. Legally Enforceable Liability The cheque must have been issued towards discharge, wholly or partly, of a legally enforceable debt or other liability.
  3. Valid Presentation The cheque must be presented within the period prescribed under the Act or its applicable validity period, whichever is earlier.
  4. Dishonour of Cheque The cheque must have been returned unpaid in circumstances attracting Section 138.
  5. Statutory Demand Notice The payee or holder in due course must make a written demand for payment within thirty days of receiving information from the bank regarding dishonour.
  6. Failure to Make Payment The drawer must fail to pay the cheque amount within fifteen days of receipt of the statutory demand notice.

A complaint must thereafter be filed in accordance with Section 142 and the applicable procedural requirements.

4. Statutory Deadlines in Cheque Bounce Cases

Compliance with statutory timelines is fundamental to proceedings under Section 138.

3 Months

Presentation of Cheque

Under the applicable RBI banking directions, cheques ordinarily remain valid for three months from the date of the instrument.

30 Days

Issuance of Legal Notice

A written demand notice must be issued within thirty days of receipt of information from the bank regarding dishonour.

15 Days

Opportunity to Make Payment

The drawer is allowed fifteen days from receipt of the statutory notice to make payment of the cheque amount.

1 Month

Filing of Criminal Complaint

The complaint must ordinarily be filed within one month from the date on which the cause of action arises under Section 138(c).

One Month Is Not Necessarily Thirty Days

Section 142(1)(b) prescribes a period of one month for filing the complaint. The applicable rules governing computation of limitation must be considered. The court may condone delay in filing the complaint upon sufficient cause being established. This power does not automatically extend the statutory notice period.

5. Legal Demand Notice Under Section 138

A written statutory demand notice is an essential prerequisite to proceedings under Section 138.

The notice must demand payment of the cheque amount and comply with the statutory requirements.

Important Particulars of a Legal Notice

  • Details of the drawer and payee.
  • Relevant facts concerning the underlying debt or liability.
  • Cheque number, date and amount.
  • Details of the concerned bank.
  • Date and reason for dishonour.
  • A clear demand for payment of the cheque amount.
  • Reference to the statutory fifteen-day payment period.

Service of Legal Notice

Proper dispatch and service of the statutory notice are important evidentiary considerations.

Postal receipts, tracking records, acknowledgment cards and returned postal envelopes may be relevant in determining service.

In appropriate circumstances, statutory presumptions concerning service may arise where notice has been properly addressed, prepaid and dispatched.

The Supreme Court considered principles concerning service of notice in C.C. Alavi Haji v. Palapetty Muhammed, (2007) 6 SCC 555.

Refusal to Accept Notice

Refusal or non-acceptance of a properly dispatched notice does not necessarily defeat proceedings. The court must examine the facts, postal records and applicable presumptions concerning service.

6. Territorial Jurisdiction Under Section 142

Section 142 of the Negotiable Instruments Act governs cognizance, limitation and territorial jurisdiction in cheque dishonour proceedings.

Under Section 142(2), jurisdiction depends upon the manner in which the cheque is presented for payment.

Mode of Presentation Territorial Jurisdiction
Cheque delivered for collection through an account Court within whose jurisdiction the branch where the payee or holder in due course maintains the account is situated.
Cheque presented otherwise than through an account Court within whose jurisdiction the branch of the drawee bank where the drawer maintains the account is situated.

The explanation to Section 142(2) and the provisions of Section 142A may also be relevant in determining the appropriate court.

A complaint under Section 138 must be instituted by the payee or holder in due course, subject to the applicable legal requirements.

No court inferior to that of a Metropolitan Magistrate or Judicial Magistrate First Class, as specified in Section 142, may try the offence. The corresponding current procedural framework and local court allocation must also be considered.

For proceedings in Ahmedabad, jurisdiction must be determined with reference to the relevant bank branch, statutory provisions and applicable court allocation.

7. Statutory Presumptions Under Sections 118 and 139

The Negotiable Instruments Act recognises certain rebuttable statutory presumptions.

Section 118(a) — Consideration

Section 118(a) provides a presumption concerning consideration in respect of negotiable instruments, unless the contrary is proved.

Section 139 — Presumption in Favour of Holder

Section 139 provides a presumption, unless the contrary is proved, that the holder received the cheque towards discharge, wholly or partly, of a debt or other liability.

In Rangappa v. Sri Mohan, (2010) 11 SCC 441, the Supreme Court held that the presumption under Section 139 extends to the existence of a legally enforceable debt or liability.

Rebuttal of Presumption

The statutory presumption is rebuttable. The accused may establish a probable defence on the standard of preponderance of probabilities.

The accused may rely upon material produced by the complainant, cross-examination and other admissible evidence.

The accused is not necessarily required to enter the witness box to rebut the presumption.

Relevant principles were discussed in Basalingappa v. Mudibasappa, (2019) 5 SCC 418.

8. Legal Defences Available to the Accused

An accused person in proceedings under Section 138 is entitled to contest the complaint in accordance with applicable law.

Depending upon the facts and evidence, relevant issues may include:

  • Absence of a legally enforceable debt or liability.
  • Discharge or repayment of the underlying liability.
  • Non-compliance with statutory notice requirements.
  • Premature institution of the complaint.
  • Limitation and absence of legally sufficient condonation.
  • Lack of territorial jurisdiction.
  • Disputed execution or signature, where supported by evidence.
  • Material inconsistencies in the complainant's evidence.
  • Financial capacity of the complainant, where genuinely placed in issue.
  • Other legally sustainable defences arising from the circumstances of the case.

Security Cheques

Merely describing an instrument as a security cheque does not automatically exclude liability under Section 138.

The relevant question includes whether a legally enforceable debt or liability had crystallised when the cheque became payable and was presented.

The Supreme Court examined this issue in Sripati Singh v. State of Jharkhand, 2021 SCC OnLine SC 1002.

Signed Blank Cheques

Voluntary delivery of a signed blank cheque does not, by itself, invalidate the statutory presumptions.

The circumstances surrounding issuance, authority, completion and the underlying liability must be examined.

Relevant principles were considered in Bir Singh v. Mukesh Kumar, (2019) 4 SCC 197.

9. Cheque Dishonour by Companies and Firms

Section 141 of the Negotiable Instruments Act deals with offences committed by companies and other entities falling within its scope.

Subject to the statutory requirements, liability may extend to the company and persons who were in charge of and responsible to the company for the conduct of its business at the relevant time.

Liability may also arise in circumstances involving consent, connivance or neglect attributable to specified officers.

Mere designation as a director does not automatically establish criminal liability in every case. The statutory requirements and necessary pleadings must be satisfied.

In Aneeta Hada v. Godfather Travels & Tours Pvt. Ltd., (2012) 5 SCC 661, the Supreme Court considered the requirement of arraigning the company for prosecution based on vicarious liability under Section 141, subject to recognised legal exceptions.

10. General Trial Procedure

Proceedings under Section 138 are governed by the special provisions of the Negotiable Instruments Act and applicable criminal procedural law.

  1. Filing of Complaint The complainant presents a written complaint before the competent court along with relevant documents.
  2. Cognizance and Process The court considers the complaint and supporting material in accordance with applicable procedural law.
  3. Appearance of Accused Upon issuance of process, the accused appears before the court and complies with applicable procedural and bail requirements.
  4. Recording of Plea The substance of accusation is communicated to the accused, and the plea is recorded.
  5. Complainant's Evidence Evidence may be presented by affidavit under Section 145, subject to the applicable requirements.
  6. Cross-Examination The accused may seek examination of the complainant or other affidavit witnesses in accordance with Section 145(2).
  7. Defence and Arguments The accused may present a legally permissible defence and relevant evidence. Both parties may advance their legal submissions.
  8. Judgment The court determines whether the statutory offence has been established and passes an appropriate judgment.

Summary Trial Under Section 143

Section 143 provides for summary trial of offences under the Act, subject to the statutory provisions and the power of the court to adopt an appropriate trial procedure where legally required.

The Act requires an endeavour to conclude the trial within six months from the date of filing of the complaint. This is not an unconditional guarantee of disposal within that period.

Bank Return Memo Under Section 146

Section 146 provides that a bank slip or memo bearing the official mark denoting dishonour shall constitute prima facie evidence of dishonour unless the contrary is proved.

11. Interim Compensation Under Section 143A

Section 143A empowers the court trying an offence under Section 138 to direct payment of interim compensation in circumstances specified under the provision.

In a summary trial or summons case, such an order may be considered where the accused pleads not guilty. In other cases, the provision refers to the stage following framing of charge.

Particulars Statutory Position
Maximum interim compensation Not exceeding 20% of the cheque amount.
Ordinary payment period 60 days from the date of the order.
Permissible extension Up to a further 30 days upon sufficient cause being shown.
Acquittal of drawer Repayment with interest at the applicable RBI bank rate, in accordance with Section 143A(4).
Interim Compensation Is Discretionary

Section 143A does not mandate an automatic award of twenty per cent in every case. The court must exercise judicial discretion and consider relevant circumstances, as explained in Rakesh Ranjan Shrivastava v. State of Jharkhand, 2024 SCC OnLine SC 309.

The temporal applicability of Section 143A must also be considered in light of G.J. Raja v. Tejraj Surana, (2019) 19 SCC 469.

12. Punishment and Compensation Under Section 138

Upon conviction under Section 138, the court may impose:

  • Imprisonment for a term which may extend to two years.
  • Fine which may extend to twice the amount of the cheque.
  • Both imprisonment and fine.

The sentence is determined by the court in accordance with applicable law and the individual circumstances of the case.

Compensation may also be awarded in accordance with the applicable statutory and procedural provisions.

Section 138 does not prescribe mandatory imprisonment in every case, nor does it automatically require an award of twice the cheque amount.

13. Appeal and Deposit Under Section 148

Section 148 empowers the appellate court, in an appeal by the drawer against conviction under Section 138, to direct deposit of a sum which shall be a minimum of twenty per cent of the fine or compensation awarded by the trial court, where such a deposit is ordered.

Such deposit is in addition to any interim compensation paid under Section 143A.

The amount is ordinarily required to be deposited within sixty days from the date of the order, with a possible extension of up to thirty days upon sufficient cause being shown.

Judicial interpretation recognises that an appellate court may dispense with the deposit requirement in exceptional circumstances, upon recording appropriate reasons.

Relevant judicial authorities include Surinder Singh Deswal v. Virender Gandhi, (2019) 11 SCC 341 and Jamboo Bhandari v. M.P. State Industrial Development Corporation Ltd., (2023) 10 SCC 446.

14. Settlement and Compounding Under Section 147

Section 147 of the Negotiable Instruments Act provides that offences punishable under the Act are compoundable.

Parties may explore an amicable settlement, subject to the applicable legal requirements, consent and appropriate orders of the competent court.

Settlement may involve payment of the outstanding amount, agreed instalments, compensation or other lawful terms.

Compounding may be considered at different stages of proceedings, subject to applicable judicial principles and requirements.

The Supreme Court considered principles concerning compounding and graded costs in Damodar S. Prabhu v. Sayed Babalal H., (2010) 5 SCC 663.

Any settlement should clearly address the manner of payment, consequences of default and disposal of pending proceedings.

15. Civil Recovery and Criminal Proceedings

Proceedings under Section 138 are distinct from civil proceedings for recovery of money.

Depending upon the nature of the underlying transaction and applicable law, an aggrieved party may also have a civil remedy for recovery.

Such remedies may include:

  • Ordinary civil recovery proceedings.
  • Summary proceedings under Order XXXVII of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, where maintainable.
  • Commercial dispute proceedings, where the statutory requirements are satisfied.
  • Arbitration proceedings, where a valid arbitration agreement and arbitrable dispute exist.

Each remedy has its own jurisdictional, procedural and limitation requirements. Amounts already recovered must be appropriately accounted for to avoid double recovery.

16. Documents Commonly Required

Documentary requirements depend upon the circumstances of the transaction and the nature of the proceedings.

  • Original dishonoured cheque.
  • Bank return memo.
  • Copy of the statutory demand notice.
  • Postal receipts and dispatch records.
  • Postal tracking reports and acknowledgment records.
  • Returned postal envelope, where applicable.
  • Relevant bank statements.
  • Loan agreements, invoices, receipts or contractual records.
  • Relevant account statements and ledger records.
  • Communications concerning the underlying liability.
  • Authorisation documents, where proceedings are instituted through an authorised representative.
  • Other admissible evidence supporting the claim or defence.

Electronic records must comply with the applicable requirements of evidence law, including certification where legally required.

17. Frequently Asked Questions

Is cheque bounce a criminal offence?

Cheque dishonour may constitute an offence under Section 138 where all statutory ingredients and procedural requirements are satisfied.

Is a legal notice compulsory?

Yes. A written statutory demand notice complying with Section 138 is an essential prerequisite to prosecution under that provision.

What is the time limit for issuing a notice?

The statutory demand notice must be issued within thirty days from receipt of information from the bank regarding dishonour of the cheque.

Can a complaint be filed immediately after issuing a legal notice?

No. The statutory fifteen-day payment period following receipt of notice must expire without payment before the cause of action arises.

What happens if the drawer refuses to accept the legal notice?

Refusal may constitute deemed service in appropriate circumstances. Proper dispatch, address details, postal records and applicable legal presumptions must be considered.

Can a cheque be presented again after dishonour?

A cheque may be presented again within its validity period, subject to applicable law. A subsequent dishonour may give rise to a fresh cause of action upon compliance with the statutory requirements.

Can a security cheque attract Section 138?

Yes, where a legally enforceable liability has crystallised and the other statutory requirements are satisfied. The description of the cheque as security is not conclusive.

Is a signed blank cheque legally enforceable?

Voluntary issuance of a signed blank cheque does not automatically defeat proceedings. The circumstances of issuance, underlying liability and statutory presumptions must be examined.

Is imprisonment compulsory in every case?

No. Section 138 provides for imprisonment, fine or both. The appropriate sentence is determined by the competent court.

Is a cheque bounce offence bailable?

An offence under Section 138 alone is ordinarily bailable and non-cognizable under the applicable criminal procedural classification.

Is twenty per cent interim compensation mandatory?

No. An order under Section 143A is discretionary and cannot exceed twenty per cent of the cheque amount. The court must consider relevant circumstances.

Can cheque bounce proceedings be settled?

Yes. Section 147 provides for compounding of offences, subject to applicable legal requirements and appropriate orders of the court.

Can a civil recovery suit and Section 138 complaint proceed simultaneously?

Distinct civil and criminal remedies may coexist where legally maintainable. Their respective statutory requirements must be satisfied, and duplicate recovery is not permissible.

Can a delayed cheque bounce complaint be filed?

Section 142 permits the court to condone delay in filing a complaint upon sufficient cause being established. This does not automatically cure non-compliance with other statutory requirements.
