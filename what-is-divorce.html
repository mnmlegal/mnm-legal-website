Divorce Law in India: A Comprehensive Legal Guide

Understanding the legal dissolution of marriage, statutory grounds for divorce, matrimonial remedies and the procedure before competent courts in India.

MNM Legal Associates Family Law Legal Guide

Marriage creates legal rights and obligations between spouses. Where a matrimonial relationship can no longer continue, the applicable law may provide remedies including judicial separation, restitution of conjugal rights and divorce.

Divorce proceedings involve more than the dissolution of marriage. Depending upon the circumstances, questions relating to maintenance, permanent alimony, child custody, property and other matrimonial rights may also require determination.

1. What Is Divorce?

Divorce is the legal dissolution of a valid marriage in accordance with the applicable matrimonial law.

Under statutes such as the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 and the Special Marriage Act, 1954, dissolution ordinarily requires a decree of divorce from a competent court.

The legal requirements and recognised modes of dissolution may differ under other applicable personal laws.

Mere separation between spouses does not, by itself, ordinarily terminate a marriage.

2. Laws Governing Divorce in India

India has different statutory and personal law frameworks governing marriage and divorce. The applicable law depends upon the legal circumstances of the marriage.

Applicable Law General Application
Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 Hindus, Buddhists, Jains and Sikhs, subject to statutory applicability.
Special Marriage Act, 1954 Marriages governed by the Special Marriage Act.
Divorce Act, 1869 Christian matrimonial proceedings, subject to statutory requirements.
Parsi Marriage and Divorce Act, 1936 Parsi matrimonial matters.
Muslim Personal Law and Applicable Statutes Muslim matrimonial matters, including applicable statutory remedies.
Important Legal Distinction

The following discussion primarily concerns proceedings under the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955. Its provisions should not be automatically applied to marriages governed by other laws.

3. Mutual Consent Divorce

Section 13B of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 provides for divorce by mutual consent.

Both spouses jointly seek dissolution of their marriage upon satisfying the applicable statutory requirements.

Statutory Requirements

  • The parties have been living separately for a period of one year or more.
  • They have been unable to live together.
  • They have mutually agreed that the marriage should be dissolved.
  • Their consent must be free and voluntary.

Matters Commonly Addressed in Settlement

  • Permanent alimony and maintenance.
  • Child custody and visitation arrangements.
  • Return of stridhan and personal belongings.
  • Financial and property-related arrangements.
  • Lawful resolution of pending proceedings.

Six-Month Waiting Period

Section 13B(2) ordinarily provides for a second motion not earlier than six months and not later than eighteen months after presentation of the joint petition, provided the petition has not been withdrawn.

In Amardeep Singh v. Harveen Kaur, (2017) 8 SCC 746, the Supreme Court held that the six-month waiting period under Section 13B(2) is directory and may be waived by the competent court in appropriate circumstances.

Such waiver is not automatic. It depends upon the circumstances of the case and the satisfaction of the court.

4. Contested Divorce

Contested divorce proceedings arise where one spouse seeks dissolution of marriage on a legally recognised ground, without requiring the consent of the other spouse.

Under the Hindu Marriage Act, such proceedings may be instituted under Section 13, subject to the applicable statutory grounds and requirements.

The court considers the pleadings, evidence, statutory provisions and relevant judicial precedents before determining whether the petitioner is entitled to a decree of divorce.

5. Grounds for Divorce Under the Hindu Marriage Act

Section 13 of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 recognises various grounds for divorce, including the following.

Adultery

Voluntary sexual intercourse by a spouse with a person other than their spouse after solemnisation of the marriage constitutes a statutory ground for divorce under Section 13(1)(i).

Although adultery was decriminalised in Joseph Shine v. Union of India, (2019) 3 SCC 39, it continues to remain a recognised matrimonial ground for divorce.

Cruelty

Section 13(1)(ia) recognises cruelty as a ground for divorce. Cruelty may be physical or mental, depending upon the nature, gravity and cumulative effect of the conduct established before the court.

Desertion

Under Section 13(1)(ib), desertion for a continuous period of not less than two years immediately preceding presentation of the petition may constitute a ground for divorce.

Mere physical separation is not necessarily sufficient. The statutory ingredients of desertion must be established.

Conversion

Conversion by a spouse to another religion, resulting in that spouse ceasing to be Hindu, is a recognised ground under Section 13(1)(ii).

Mental Disorder

Section 13(1)(iii) provides a ground relating to incurable unsoundness of mind or mental disorder of the nature and extent specified in the provision.

A medical diagnosis alone does not automatically establish entitlement to divorce.

Other Statutory Grounds

The Act also recognises certain other grounds, including communicable venereal disease, renunciation of the world and absence of information regarding a person's existence for seven years or more, subject to the statutory conditions.

Section 13(1A) provides additional grounds following certain decrees of judicial separation or restitution of conjugal rights. Section 13(2) provides specified additional grounds available to a wife.

Irretrievable Breakdown of Marriage

Irretrievable breakdown of marriage is not an independent statutory ground available to ordinary Family Courts under Section 13 of the Hindu Marriage Act. The Supreme Court may exercise its exceptional jurisdiction under Article 142 of the Constitution in appropriate cases, as explained in Shilpa Sailesh v. Varun Sreenivasan, (2023) 14 SCC 231.

6. Which Court Has Jurisdiction?

Section 19 of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 specifies the territorial jurisdiction for matrimonial petitions under the Act.

Depending upon the circumstances, a petition may be presented before the competent court within whose territorial jurisdiction:

  • The marriage was solemnised.
  • The respondent resides at the time of presentation of the petition.
  • The parties last resided together.
  • The wife resides at the time of presentation, where she is the petitioner.
  • The petitioner resides in certain exceptional circumstances specified under Section 19(iv).

Where a Family Court has been established for the relevant territorial area, the jurisdiction conferred under Sections 7 and 8 of the Family Courts Act, 1984 must also be considered.

For matrimonial proceedings in Ahmedabad, the appropriate Family Court or other competent court must be identified on the basis of the applicable statutory jurisdiction and the facts of the marriage.

7. General Procedure for Divorce Proceedings

  1. Identification of Applicable Law Determine the governing matrimonial statute, available grounds and appropriate jurisdiction.
  2. Preparation of Petition Prepare the matrimonial petition with relevant facts, statutory provisions, reliefs and supporting documents.
  3. Institution of Proceedings Present the petition before the competent court in accordance with applicable filing requirements.
  4. Notice and Appearance In contested proceedings, notice is ordinarily issued to the respondent, who may appear and submit a response.
  5. Reconciliation or Mediation Where appropriate, the court may undertake or facilitate settlement and reconciliation efforts in accordance with applicable law.
  6. Evidence and Arguments In contested matters, the parties may lead evidence, conduct cross-examination and advance legal submissions.
  7. Judgment and Decree The court determines the petition and grants or refuses the reliefs sought in accordance with law.

Mutual consent proceedings follow a different procedure, including the statutory requirements concerning joint presentation, motions and continuing consent.

8. Maintenance and Permanent Alimony

Maintenance and alimony are financial remedies intended to address legally recognised support obligations.

Section 24 — Hindu Marriage Act

Section 24 provides for maintenance pendente lite and expenses of proceedings where either spouse lacks independent income sufficient for their support and necessary litigation expenses.

Section 25 — Hindu Marriage Act

Section 25 empowers the court to grant permanent alimony and maintenance, subject to the statutory considerations and circumstances of the parties.

Section 144 — BNSS, 2023

Section 144 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 provides a statutory maintenance remedy for eligible wives, children and parents, subject to the conditions specified in the provision.

The provision broadly corresponds to Section 125 of the former Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, subject to applicable transitional provisions.

Determination of Maintenance

Courts may consider income, assets, liabilities, reasonable needs, standard of living, dependent family members and other relevant circumstances.

There is no universal fixed percentage of income applicable to every maintenance or alimony proceeding.

For further information, see our guide on maintenance law in India .

9. Child Custody and Visitation Rights

Matrimonial proceedings may involve questions concerning the custody, education, maintenance and welfare of minor children.

Section 26 of the Hindu Marriage Act, the Guardians and Wards Act, 1890 and other applicable statutory provisions may be relevant to such proceedings.

The welfare and best interests of the child remain the paramount consideration in determining custody arrangements.

Depending upon the circumstances, courts may consider:

  • The child's age and developmental needs.
  • Emotional and physical well-being.
  • Educational continuity and stability.
  • The ability of each parent to provide care.
  • The child's wishes, where appropriate.
  • Visitation and access arrangements.

Neither parent has an unconditional entitlement to custody merely because of their status as father or mother.

10. Documents Commonly Required

Documentary requirements vary depending upon the nature of the proceedings, the reliefs sought and local court rules.

  • Identity and address proof of the parties.
  • Marriage certificate, where available.
  • Marriage photographs or other proof of solemnisation.
  • Relevant residential documents establishing jurisdiction.
  • Income and financial documents, where maintenance is involved.
  • Birth certificates and relevant records concerning minor children.
  • Relevant correspondence, notices and supporting evidence.
  • Settlement terms, where applicable.
  • Copies of relevant pending or concluded court proceedings.

The admissibility and evidentiary value of documents must be assessed in accordance with applicable evidence law.

11. Other Matrimonial Remedies

Divorce is not the only remedy available in matrimonial disputes.

Restitution of Conjugal Rights

Section 9 of the Hindu Marriage Act provides for restitution of conjugal rights where one spouse has withdrawn from the society of the other without reasonable excuse, subject to the statutory requirements.

Judicial Separation

Section 10 provides for judicial separation. A decree of judicial separation does not itself dissolve the marriage, but removes the obligation to cohabit while the decree remains operative.

Domestic Violence Remedies

The Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005 provides various remedies to eligible aggrieved women, including protection orders, residence orders, monetary relief, custody orders and compensation, subject to the applicable statutory requirements.

Annulment of Marriage

Sections 11 and 12 of the Hindu Marriage Act deal with void and voidable marriages respectively, subject to their distinct statutory grounds and requirements.

12. Frequently Asked Questions

Can divorce be filed within one year of marriage?

Section 14 of the Hindu Marriage Act ordinarily prohibits presentation of a divorce petition within one year of marriage. The court may grant leave in cases of exceptional hardship to the petitioner or exceptional depravity on the part of the respondent.

Can one spouse obtain divorce without the other's consent?

Yes. Contested divorce may be granted without mutual consent where a legally recognised ground is established and the applicable statutory conditions are satisfied.

Is the six-month waiting period compulsory in mutual consent divorce?

Under Section 13B(2) of the Hindu Marriage Act, the competent court may waive the six-month waiting period in appropriate circumstances. Such waiver is discretionary and not automatic.

How long does a contested divorce case take?

The duration depends upon the nature of the dispute, service of notice, evidence, interim applications, court workload and other procedural factors. No uniform disposal period can be guaranteed.

Can maintenance be claimed after divorce?

Maintenance or permanent alimony may be available after divorce under applicable statutory provisions, subject to eligibility, existing orders, settlement terms and the circumstances of the case.

Does the mother automatically receive child custody?

No. Custody is determined primarily on the basis of the child's welfare and best interests, taking into account applicable statutory provisions and relevant circumstances.

Can a divorce petition be withdrawn?

Withdrawal may be permissible subject to the applicable law, procedural requirements and stage of proceedings. In mutual consent divorce, continuing consent is ordinarily necessary until the decree is passed.

Is a marriage certificate mandatory for filing divorce?

A marriage certificate is important evidence of marriage. However, its absence does not necessarily prevent proceedings where the marriage can otherwise be established in accordance with applicable law and court requirements.

When can a divorced person remarry?

Section 15 of the Hindu Marriage Act governs remarriage following a decree of divorce. The right of appeal, expiry of the applicable appeal period and disposal of any appeal must be considered before remarriage.
